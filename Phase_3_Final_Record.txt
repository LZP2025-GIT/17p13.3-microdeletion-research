Phase 3A - Abstract and Methods Summary

Abstract-Ready Phase 3 Summary

Phase 3 was organized into two analytical parts to determine whether the molecular

       mechanisms identified in Phase 2 have the spatial opportunity to operate within the

human visual system. Part I — retinal and ocular localization combined adult whole-tissue

screening with adult human retinal cell-type analysis. Bulk retinal data showed the strongest

expression among affected protein-coding loci for PRPF8, SERPINF1, PITPNA, RPA1,

INPP5K, and TLCD2. Cell-type resolution then identified the clearest spatial convergence in

retinal pigment epithelium, where INPP5K, PITPNA, and SERPINF1 converge on

phosphoinositide regulation, phospholipid handling, and PEDF-associated trophic/lipid

signaling. PRPF8 was broadly represented across neural retina, including photoreceptors, while

TLCD2 showed a preliminary cone-photoreceptor signal. In contrast, the predicted WDR81-

RILP trafficking pair was not demonstrated as a shared adult-retinal neuronal module. Part II —

central visual-relay

specificity tested whether the affected interval preferentially mapped onto magnocellular,

       parvocellular, or koniocellular transcriptional programs in the human dorsal lateral

geniculate nucleus. None of the 17 affected loci appeared among the reproducible human M-

versus-P marker genes or principal K-cell markers reported in the reference dataset. Phase 3

therefore provides evidence about where affected loci are expressed, which visual-system cell
                                                                                                   81

populations can plausibly experience their molecular effects, and whether the deletion

preferentially maps onto a specific visual relay stream. The evidence to date supports a

       distributed retinal and neural functional-reserve model, with the strongest cell-specific

signal centered on RPE membrane/lipid biology, but does not support a selectively

magnocellular lesion or establish a definitive visual-neurological phenotype.

What Phase 3 Provides

Phase 2 established mechanistic plausibility by determining what the affected loci

       normally do. Phase 3 adds spatial opportunity: it tests whether those loci are normally

active in the tissues and cell populations required for the proposed mechanisms to matter in the

human visual system. Through the work completed to date, Phase 3 distinguishes broad retinal

expression from cell-specific localization, tests whether molecularly related deleted genes are

actually co-located in the same retinal compartment, and directly evaluates whether the deletion

preferentially corresponds to one classical thalamic visual pathway. It therefore narrows the

hypothesis without treating expression as proof of dysfunction.

Step-by-Step Method Used Through Phase 3D

Step 1 — Freeze the candidate set and hypotheses before examining expression results

       All 17 structurally affected loci identified in Phase 1 were retained. No gene was

removed because Phase 2 had assigned it weak visual or neurological relevance. The primary

Phase 3 hypotheses were frozen in advance, including membrane/endosomal co-

localization,

       direct retinal localization, WDR81-RILP overlap, developmental enrichment,

magnocellular specificity, and regulatory overlap. This was done to reduce phenotype-driven

selection and confirmation bias.
                                                                                                   82

Step 2 — Separate loci according to assay compatibility

       Protein-coding genes were treated as generally measurable by conventional bulk and

single-cell RNA sequencing. PITPNA-AS1 and MIR22HG were retained with the recognition

that lncRNAs can be poorly captured in some single-cell platforms. Mature MIR22 was not

interpreted from ordinary poly(A)-selected RNA-seq because mature microRNAs require small-

RNA or miRNA-specific measurement. RN7SL105P was treated cautiously because pseudogene

homology can cause ambiguous short-read mapping.

Step 3 — Screen adult whole-body tissue expression

       Adult expression resources including GTEx and NCBI Gene summaries were used to

determine whether each affected protein-coding locus was broadly expressed, brain biased,

peripheral-tissue enriched, or strongly tissue specific. This established general biological context

before any retina-specific ranking was performed. Cross-platform expression magnitudes were

not treated as directly interchangeable.

Step 4 — Perform the initial adult human retinal screen

       Human ocular resources, especially eyeIntegration and the Human Protein Atlas retinal

series, were used to determine which affected genes were detectably expressed in adult retina.

The strongest bulk retinal signals among the affected protein-coding genes were PRPF8,

SERPINF1, PITPNA, RPA1, INPP5K, and TLCD2. Genes showing weak whole-retina

abundance were not discarded because bulk measurements can dilute expression from rare cell

populations.

Step 5 — Resolve the retinal signal to individual cell classes

       Large human retinal single-cell atlases and independent adult human retinal datasets were

used to test localization among photoreceptors, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, horizontal cells,
                                                                                              83

retinal ganglion cells, Müller glia, astrocytes, microglia, vascular populations, and RPE.

Published marker frameworks were used as quality-control references for identifying major

retinal populations.

Step 6 — Test whether the Phase 2 molecular convergence survives cell-type resolution

       The pre-specified membrane/endosomal candidate set was examined for shared

localization. The strongest supported adult-retinal convergence became an RPE-centered

INPP5K-PITPNA-SERPINF1 module. PRPF8 was broadly present across neural retina,

and

       TLCD2 showed a preliminary cone-associated signal. Conversely, WDR81 and RILP

were not demonstrated as a shared mature retinal neuronal module. Negative results were

retained rather than redefining the candidate set after inspection.

Step 7 — Move from retina to the central visual relay

       Human dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus single-nucleus RNA-seq data from the

Allen/Bakken comparative LGN resource were used to test the pre-specified magnocellular

hypothesis. The analysis treated magnocellular, parvocellular, and koniocellular identities

symmetrically rather than defining magnocellular enrichment as the desired result.

Step 8 — Compare affected loci with published human M/P/K transcriptional markers

       The human dLGN study showed that M and P projection neurons largely form one

transcriptomic MP population with a graded M-to-P continuum. The published reproducible M/P

marker set and principal K-cell markers were compared against all 17 affected loci. None of the

affected loci was a defining M, P, or K marker. The result was therefore recorded as a

meaningful negative finding: current adult human dLGN transcriptomics does not support

preferential magnocellular targeting by the deletion.
                                                                                                  84

Step 9 — Update the model without converting expression into causality

       The evidence from the retinal and dLGN analyses was integrated with the Phase 2

mechanisms. Expression was interpreted only as opportunity for a mechanism, not proof that the

deletion produces dysfunction. The working visual-system model therefore shifted away from a

selectively magnocellular lesion toward a distributed retinal and neural functional-

reserve model, with the strongest present spatial convergence in RPE. No definitive

phenotype

       was assigned.

Current Phase 3 Interpretation

       Through Phase 3D, the study has established that several molecularly important deleted

genes are normally positioned within biologically relevant human visual-system compartments.

The strongest cell-type-specific convergence is INPP5K + PITPNA + SERPINF1 in RPE, while

PRPF8 has broad neural-retinal availability and TLCD2 may contribute a cone-associated lipid

component. At the same time, the data weaken two earlier possibilities: WDR81 and RILP have

not been demonstrated as a shared adult-retinal trafficking module, and none of the affected loci

demonstrates published magnocellular-specific transcriptional enrichment in adult human dLGN.

The Phase 3 evidence therefore supports continued investigation of a distributed retinal/neural

mechanism while rejecting, at the present level of evidence, a specifically magnocellular

transcriptomic explanation.

Phase 3 remains in progress. Human V1 and developmental brain/retinal analyses have

       not yet been completed and must not be represented as results.

Phase 3A — Adult Whole-Tissue and Initial Ocular Expression Screen

1. Objective

Phase 3A asks whether the 17 loci affected by the 17p13.3 microdeletion are

        transcriptionally active in adult human tissues relevant to the project's visual-neurological

hypothesis.

        The purpose is not to infer disease from expression.

        Instead, Phase 3A establishes the first requirement for biological plausibility:

A deleted locus must normally be expressed in the relevant tissue before

reduced dosage can plausibly perturb that tissue through its canonical

        molecular function.

        All 17 loci remain under consideration.

provides gene-level TPM and median tissue-expression matrices, including the current V11

median gene-level TPM file. ("GTEx Portal")

       The first result is that the deleted interval does not consist predominantly of retinaspecific

or brain-specific genes.

       Most affected protein-coding genes are broadly or ubiquitously expressed.

       This is methodologically important.

       A visual-neurological mechanism, if present, will therefore probably depend on:

cell type + developmental context + dosage sensitivity

       rather than simply: brain-specific gene expression.

Phase 3A demonstrates that several of the strongest Phase 2 candidate mechanisms

       possess clear spatial opportunity within adult human retina.

       Most notably:

PITPNA

INPP5K

TLCD2

       and:

SERPINF1

       are simultaneously represented in adult retinal tissue, supporting the existence of an

ocular membrane/lipid-regulatory subnetwork.

       PRPF8 is strongly expressed in the same gross tissue, while RPA1 is also substantially

expressed and RTN4RL1 is clearly detectable.
                                                                                                 106

         Conversely, WDR81, RILP, SCARF1, SERPINF2, and SLC43A2 show low whole-retina

imply adult retinal importance.

         The appropriate conclusion is therefore:

Phase 3A provides preliminary spatial support for a subset of the Phase 2

visual-neural model, especially the PITPNA–INPP5K–TLCD2–SERPINF1

membrane/lipid axis, while leaving the WDR81–RILP trafficking component

unresolved and weakening direct retinal prioritization of several systemic

         genes.

         The next analysis must move below the tissue level.

         Whole-retina RNA cannot determine whether these genes occur in the same biological

cells.

         The decisive next question is:

Which retinal cell populations—rods, cones, bipolar cells, amacrine cells,

retinal ganglion cells, Müller glia, RPE, vascular cells, or immune cells—

express each affected locus?

Phase 3C — Adult Human Retinal Single-Cell Localization

1. Objective

Phase 3A established that several affected genes are detectably expressed in adult human

         retina at the whole-tissue level.

Phase 3C asks the more discriminating question:

Which adult human retinal cell populations actually express the affected loci,

and do the Phase 2 mechanistically related genes occupy the same cellular

compartments?

         This distinction is essential because bulk retinal RNA-seq can generate apparent

convergence even when different genes are expressed in completely different cell populations.
                                                                                                  108

        The principal cell classes considered are:

rod photoreceptors, cone photoreceptors, bipolar cells, amacrine cells, horizontal

cells, retinal ganglion cells, Müller glia, astrocytes, microglia, and retinal pigment epithelial

        cells.

        RPE is treated separately from the neural retina because it is an epithelial support layer

rather than a retinal neuron, despite its critical role in photoreceptor physiology.

2. Reference Data

        The principal contemporary reference is the 2026 Human Retina Cell Atlas, which

integrated approximately 3.9 million cells from 125 human donors and identified more than

130 retinal cell types. The atlas integrates multiple published studies with approximately

2.7

        million previously unpublished observations and includes both transcriptomic and

chromatinaccessibility information. (Li et al.)

        For replication and interpretation, older independent human retinal datasets remain

useful. The 2019 Lukowski atlas profiled 20,009 cells from three healthy adult donors and

resolved all major neural retinal populations, including rods, cones, bipolar cells, amacrine cells,

retinal ganglion cells, horizontal cells, Müller glia, astrocytes, and microglia. (Lukowski et al.)

        The current gene-level screen uses accessible Human Protein Atlas single-cell

annotations together with independent retinal studies. Because some HPA classifications vary

between releases and because the largest 2026 atlas is distributed primarily through interactive

and downloadable single-cell resources, release-dependent signals are classified as preliminary

rather than treated as replicated facts.

9. Why the RPE Finding Matters

       RPE cells perform unusually membrane-intensive functions.

       They support photoreceptors through:

   outer-segment phagocytosis;

   retinoid recycling;

   nutrient transport;

   ion and water regulation;

   growth-factor secretion;

   lipid handling.

       Therefore a convergence of lipid and phosphoinositide regulators within RPE is

biologically coherent.

       However, this observation must not be converted prematurely into:

       RPE dysfunction causes the candidate visual phenotype.

       At this stage the correct conclusion is narrower:

The deletion simultaneously affects several genes whose normal expression

       and molecular functions converge within RPE cells.

       That establishes a plausible cellular site of combined dosage perturbation.

10. PRPF8 Is Broadly Expressed Across Neural Retina

       Earlier HPA releases classified PRPF8 as enhanced in rod photoreceptor cells and placed

it within a photoreceptor/visual-perception expression cluster. ("PRPF8 Single Cell")

       However, more recent evidence argues against interpreting PRPF8 as primarily

rodspecific.

       A 2026 human retinal-organoid study reported PRPF8 expression throughout the neural

retina, including both rod and cone photoreceptors, and noted that this distribution is consistent

with existing human retinal single-cell data showing broadly uniform retinal expression.

(Zimmann et al.)

       Therefore the stronger interpretation is:

PRPF8 is broadly required across retinal cell populations, with robust

       photoreceptor expression, rather than being a uniquely rod-restricted gene.

       This fits its molecular role as a core spliceosomal protein.

Phase 3C status

       Adult neural-retinal expression: strong. Rod expression: strong. Cone expression: strong.

Strict rod specificity: not supported.

11. PRPF8 Therefore Represents a Different Type of Retinal Risk

       The RPE module involves cell-enriched membrane and trophic functions.

       PRPF8 is different.

       Because it is a core RNA-processing protein expressed broadly through neural retina,

reduced PRPF8 dosage—if functionally important—could potentially affect multiple retinal cell

populations simultaneously.

       Thus:

RPE module

        The four noncoding loci cannot be treated like ordinary protein-coding genes.

MIR22HG

34. Phase 3C Working Conclusion

          Adult human retinal single-cell analysis substantially refines the bulk-expression model

developed in Phase 3A.

          The strongest finding is a reproducible RPE-centered convergence involving

SERPINF1, INPP5K, and PITPNA. These genes independently regulate extracellular

retinal

          trophic signaling, phosphoinositide metabolism, and phospholipid handling, respectively.

Their shared RPE localization provides the first strong evidence that multiple deleted genes with

related membrane/lipid functions occupy the same biologically relevant human ocular cell

compartment.

          PRPF8 represents a second major retinal axis. Rather than being narrowly restricted to

rods, current evidence supports broad expression through the neural retina, including rods and

cones, consistent with a fundamental spliceosomal function.

          TLCD2 provides a preliminary cone-photoreceptor signal that may extend the

membranelipid mechanism into neural retina, but replication is required.

          Conversely, the expected WDR81–RILP endosomal pair is not currently supported as a

shared adult retinal module. WDR81 shows a more microglial-associated expression pattern,
                                                                                                 138

whereas RILP lacks strong retina-specific enrichment and retains a broader neuronal profile.

RTN4RL1 also lacks demonstrated retinal ganglion-cell enrichment despite its strong

experimental optic-nerve biology.

Phase 3C therefore supports retinal involvement while narrowing and reorganizing

       the mechanism.

       The strongest present model is no longer:

       one six-gene membrane pathway operating uniformly throughout retina.

       It is:

an RPE-centered membrane/phosphoinositide/trophic module, a separate

photoreceptor-associated RNA/lipid module, and additional neural-

trafficking genes whose principal relevance may emerge farther downstream

       in the central visual pathway or during development.

       That distinction should now be carried forward unchanged into the next analyses.

2. Biological Context

         The dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus is the principal thalamic relay between:

retina

         ↓

dLGN

parvocellular and koniocellular streams.

   morphology;

   receptive-field properties;

   temporal response;

   contrast sensitivity;

   chromatic processing;

   retinal input;

   cortical projection patterns.

       Failure to find M-specific enrichment should not be exaggerated.

       The original study itself found that human M and P neurons are remarkably similar

gradient rather than completely distinct cell types. (Bakken et al.)

       Consequently:

       gene expression.

       A deleted gene expressed equally in M and P neurons could theoretically have different

functional consequences if the two neuronal types impose different physiological demands.

       That possibility remains open.

       However, there is currently no positive transcriptomic evidence supporting it.

27. Phase 3D Working Conclusion

       Human dLGN transcriptomics does not currently support the hypothesis that the 17p13.3

microdeletion preferentially disrupts a magnocellular molecular program.

       Human magnocellular and parvocellular relay neurons themselves are transcriptionally

much more similar than their well-known physiological differences might suggest. They form a

largely shared MP transcriptomic population with a comparatively subtle M-to-P expression

gradient. A limited set of genes—including CRH, SUSD2 and ROBO2 toward the magnocellular

end and EPHA7 and FOXP2 toward the parvocellular end—defines this gradient. None of the 17

loci affected by the index deletion appears among the published reproducible M/P marker genes.

(Bakken et al.)

       Likewise, the affected loci do not overlap the principal human koniocellular markers

CALB1, CAMK2A, PRKCG and PENK.

       Accordingly:

The pre-specified magnocellular-specificity hypothesis is not supported by

       current adult human dLGN transcriptomic evidence.

       This result does not weaken the broader proposition that the deletion may affect

visualneural biology. Instead, it changes the likely mechanism.

       The strongest spatial evidence remains the RPE-centered INPP5K–PITPNA–
                                                                                                 164

SERPINF1 convergence, combined with broad retinal PRPF8 expression and additional

neural

         mechanisms that do not appear restricted to a particular thalamic visual stream.

         The current model should therefore shift from:

a magnocellular-specific defect

         toward:

a distributed retinal and neural functional-reserve model in which several

molecular systems may be altered without preferentially targeting one

         classical M/P/K visual channel.

         That is a stronger conclusion scientifically because it follows the transcriptomic evidence

rather than the original phenotype hypothesis.

Phase 3C - Integrated Expression and Spatiotemporal Analysis

         Research Scope and Status

Phase 3 evaluates where and when genes disrupted by the GRCh37/hg19

         chr17:1,411,408-1,841,103 microdeletion are expressed, with particular emphasis on the

retina, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (dLGN), primary

visual cortex (V1), and developing human neocortex. The phase does not treat expression as

evidence of disease causation. Its purpose is narrower: to determine whether genes already

identified as structurally disrupted and mechanistically plausible in Phases 1 and 2 have spatial

and temporal opportunity to influence visual-neural biology.

         The deletion interval contains thirteen protein-coding loci considered in the Phase 3

expression analysis (INPP5K, PITPNA, SLC43A2, SCARF1, RILP, PRPF8, TLCD2, WDR81,

SERPINF2, SERPINF1, SMYD4, RPA1, and RTN4RL1) and four noncoding annotations
                                                                                                165

(PITPNA-AS1, MIR22HG, MIR22, and RN7SL105P). Phase 3 preserves the structural

interpretation established previously: the deletion begins within INPP5K and ends within

RTN4RL1, with the latter losing essentially the canonical coding sequence on the affected allele.

Those structural facts are background constraints rather than expression results.

Abstract

Phase 3 integrates bulk tissue expression, single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomics,

       retinal cell-type resources, human dLGN transcriptomics, adult human brain atlases,

developmental cortical multiomics, and spatial fetal-cortex transcriptomics. The strongest direct

cell-localized convergence remains retinal and RPE-centered: INPP5K and PITPNA show

RPEassociated expression, while SERPINF1 is strongly associated with retinal pigment

epithelial cells and has an established retinal trophic context. Human Protein Atlas data classify

INPP5K as group-enriched in retinal pigment epithelial cells and PITPNA as cell-type enhanced

in retinal pigment epithelial cells; SERPINF1 is likewise group-enriched in retinal pigment

epithelial cells (Human Protein Atlas, “INPP5K”; Human Protein Atlas, “PITPNA”; Human

Protein Atlas, “SERPINF1”). PRPF8 is broadly expressed in retina rather than restricted to one

visual cell class, TLCD2 has a cone-photoreceptor signal that remains preliminary for

mechanistic interpretation, WDR81 is more compatible with microglial/non-specific cellular

expression than a mature retinal neuronal module, and RTN4RL1 is detectably expressed in

human retina but has not been demonstrated as retinal-ganglion-cell-specific (Human Protein

Atlas, “TLCD2”; Human Protein Atlas, “WDR81”; Human Protein Atlas, “RTN4RL1”).

       Central visual-system analysis does not support a magnocellular-specific model.

Singlecell and single-nucleus profiling of primate dLGN found that human magnocellular and

parvocellular projection neurons are transcriptomically similar relative to their marked
                                                                                                   166

physiological differences; the affected 17p13.3 loci do not emerge as defining published M-, P-,

or K-cell markers (Bakken et al.). Adult cortical analysis similarly supports distributed neural

expression rather than a deletion-wide V1-specific signature. Developmental analysis strengthens

evidence that selected genes have temporal opportunity during cortical development, especially

WDR81, RTN4RL1, RILP, and SERPINF1, but independent human developmental atlases

demonstrate readily detectable V1 specialization without identifying the deletion interval as a

conspicuous V1-defining program (Bhaduri et al.; Wang et al.; Qian et al.). The resulting model

is therefore one of distributed functional reserve: several deleted genes plausibly constrain

membrane, phosphoinositide, endosomal, trophic, RNA-processing, or genome-maintenance

systems at different visual-system sites and developmental stages, but the complete causal chain

from heterozygous dosage loss to biochemical disturbance to visual-neural phenotype has not

been demonstrated.

       Methods and Evidentiary Rules

Phase 3 was designed as an expression-opportunity analysis rather than a phenotype-

       association screen. Evidence was interpreted in the following order: genomic disruption,

known molecular function, tissue or cell-type expression, spatiotemporal overlap, pathway

convergence, perturbational evidence, phenotype association, and causality last. Accordingly,

detection of a transcript in retina or cortex establishes only that the gene is biologically available

in that compartment. It does not establish that the heterozygous deletion changes transcript

abundance, protein abundance, enzymatic flux, synaptic physiology, or perception.

       Adult tissue-level observations were treated as lower-resolution evidence than

cellresolved data. Single-cell and single-nucleus findings were prioritized when the relevant cell

type was represented and assay limitations were understood. Developmental comparisons were
                                                                                                 167

not interpreted as longitudinal trajectories unless the underlying study design supported that

inference. Cross-sectional BrainSpan or Allen-derived standardized expression values from

different donors and ages were therefore treated as suggestive temporal observations rather than

within-subject fold changes. BrainSpan provides RNA-seq and microarray expression data

across multiple cortical and subcortical structures and developmental periods, including prenatal

laser-microdissection resources (Allen Institute for Brain Science).

       Noncoding loci required additional caution. Standard polyadenylated or nuclear RNA

workflows are not reliable assays for mature microRNA abundance; consequently, MIR22

cannot be considered absent or low merely because it is poorly represented in ordinary

singlenucleus RNA-seq. MIR22HG and MIR22 were also treated as related host/microRNA

annotations rather than independent deletion events. PITPNA-AS1 and RN7SL105P remain

technically underdetermined in the available visual-system single-cell resources.

       Evidence labels used throughout this phase are: established, when supported directly by

the relevant human dataset or well-validated cell-resolved resource; suggestive, when supported

by cross-sectional, lower-resolution, or indirect evidence; inference, when a mechanistic

interpretation combines independent observations without direct experimental demonstration;

and unsupported, when the proposed specificity or relationship is not demonstrated by the

reviewed evidence.

Phase 3A: Adult Whole-Tissue and Ocular Expression

       The initial adult expression screen asked whether the affected genes were detectably

expressed in retina and brain before attempting cell-type localization. This screen supported

substantial adult retinal expression opportunity for PRPF8, SERPINF1, PITPNA, RPA1,

INPP5K, and TLCD2, with lower or less discriminatory whole-retina signals for several other
                                                                                                168

interval genes. Whole-tissue expression was not treated as evidence that all of these genes act in

the same retinal cell. The Human Protein Atlas retina resource illustrates the interpretive

problem: the retina expresses a large fraction of the human proteome, so bulk detection alone has

limited specificity (Human Protein Atlas, “Retina Proteome”).

       RTN4RL1 is a useful example. Current Human Protein Atlas data report detectable

retinal RNA, with a GTEx-derived consensus around 7.9 nTPM, supporting retinal opportunity

but not assigning that expression to retinal ganglion cells or another specific neuronal population

(Human Protein Atlas, “RTN4RL1”). TLCD2 likewise shows retinal RNA in bulk data, while its

single-cell annotation includes cone photoreceptor enhancement; this makes cone involvement a

reasonable candidate for follow-up, not an established cone-specific deletion mechanism

(Human Protein Atlas, “TLCD2”).

Phase 3A therefore established a permissive rather than causal result: multiple affected

       genes are present in adult ocular tissue, so retinal mechanisms cannot be excluded on

expression grounds. The phase then moved to cell-resolved localization because bulk retinal

abundance could not distinguish photoreceptor, RPE, glial, vascular, immune, or inner-retinal

contributions.

Phase 3B-3C: Retinal Cell Types and RPE-Centered Convergence

       Human retinal single-cell resources provide a substantially stronger anatomical test.

Cowan and colleagues sequenced 285,441 single cells from retinal organoids across

developmental time points and from adult human peripheral retina, fovea, pigment epithelium,

and choroid, demonstrating that retinal disease-related genes can show marked cell-type

specificity (Cowan et al.). Independent adult retinal atlases likewise resolve foveal and peripheral
                                                                                                     169

retinal cell populations at single-cell scale (Yan et al.). These datasets make it possible to ask

whether mechanistically related deletion genes actually occupy compatible retinal compartments.

       The strongest Phase 3 retinal result is the RPE-centered convergence of INPP5K,

PITPNA, and SERPINF1. Human Protein Atlas single-cell annotations classify INPP5K as

group-enriched in retinal pigment epithelial cells and place it in an RPE-associated expression

cluster. PITPNA is cell-type enhanced in RPE, while SERPINF1 is group-enriched in RPE and

decidual stromal cells (Human Protein Atlas, “INPP5K”; Human Protein Atlas, “PITPNA”;

Human Protein Atlas, “SERPINF1”). This is important because the three genes are not merely

co-expressed names: their known molecular functions occupy complementary membrane, lipid,

phosphoinositide, and trophic biology. Phase 3 does not claim that they form a single

biochemical complex or that their heterozygous loss produces a measured RPE defect. It

establishes spatial opportunity for functional convergence.

       PRPF8 presents a different pattern. It is broadly expressed in retina and is biologically

relevant to retinal disease under established variant mechanisms, but its expression is not

restricted to one RPE or photoreceptor subtype. Consequently, PRPF8 is best treated as a broadly

available RNA-processing constraint rather than evidence for the RPE membrane module. Its

presence may matter if future perturbational work demonstrates altered splicing in affected visual

cells, but expression alone cannot supply that missing step.

       TLCD2 provides a weaker but potentially informative signal. Current Human Protein

Atlas single-cell annotation lists cone photoreceptor cells among TLCD2-enhanced cell types,

while bulk retina RNA is detectable (Human Protein Atlas, “TLCD2”). This supports a

preliminary cone-associated expression opportunity. It does not establish that TLCD2 dosage
                                                                                              170

affects cone membrane composition in the deletion carrier, nor that such an effect contributes to

photophobia, pattern glare, color processing, or another visual phenotype.

          WDR81 did not produce the expected mature retinal neuronal convergence with RILP.

Human Protein Atlas annotations describe WDR81 as having low overall cell-type specificity

with enhancement in microglia among several cell types (Human Protein Atlas, “WDR81”).

RILP, by contrast, is assigned to a neuronal neurotransmission-and-signaling expression cluster

in the broader single-cell atlas (Human Protein Atlas, “RILP”). These findings do not

demonstrate a shared adult retinal neuronal trafficking module. The WDR81-RILP relationship

therefore remained mechanistically plausible from Phase 2 but anatomically unresolved in adult

retina.

          The retinal conclusion is correspondingly asymmetric. INPP5K-PITPNA-SERPINF1

represents the strongest direct cell-localized convergence in Phase 3. PRPF8 contributes broad

retinal RNA-processing opportunity; TLCD2 has preliminary cone-associated support;

RTN4RL1 is expressed in retina but lacks demonstrated retinal-ganglion-cell enrichment; and

WDR81-RILP co-localization in mature retinal neurons has not been established.

Phase 3D: Human dLGN and the Magnocellular Hypothesis

Phase 3D tested whether the deletion genes preferentially map to the magnocellular

          visual pathway. This was a particularly important falsification test because a

magnocellular model could otherwise become an attractive but weakly constrained explanation

for visual discomfort or contrast-related symptoms. Bakken and colleagues profiled primate

dLGN using single-cell and single-nucleus RNA sequencing, including nuclei from anatomically

defined human magnocellular, parvocellular, and koniocellular regions. They identified four

principal thalamocortical neuron types in primates but found relatively few strong transcriptional
                                                                                                171

differences between M and P populations despite their well-known morphological and

physiological differences (Bakken et al.).

       In human dLGN, M and P neurons largely occupy a shared transcriptomic MP cluster

with a gradient rather than two completely separate molecular classes. Published M-associated

markers include CRH and SUSD2, whereas EPHA7 is among validated P-associated markers;

the study also identifies K-cell classes separately (Bakken et al.). None of the affected 17p13.3

loci emerged as a defining published M, P, or principal K marker in this analysis.

       This is a negative result with a specific meaning. It does not show that the deletion genes

are absent from magnocellular neurons. It shows that the available human dLGN transcriptomic

evidence does not support preferential magnocellular enrichment. Because M and P neurons are

themselves transcriptomically similar, absence from a marker list cannot be treated as proof of

equal functional importance in both populations. Nevertheless, the data fail to support a

deletionwide magnocellular-specific mechanism. Hypothesis H5 is therefore classified as

unsupported.

Phase 3E: Adult Human V1 and Central Cortical Distribution

Phase 3E extended the analysis from the thalamus to human cerebral cortex, with

       particular interest in V1. Large adult human brain atlases demonstrate extensive neuronal

and glial diversity across the brain and provide a framework for distinguishing broadly expressed

genes from regionally specialized programs. Siletti and colleagues profiled more than three

million nuclei from approximately one hundred dissections and identified thousands of

transcriptomic subclusters, including area-specific cortical neurons (Siletti et al.).

       Within this framework, several affected genes have credible central-neural expression,

but the interval does not emerge as a coherent adult V1-specific program. RILP gains support as
                                                                                                   172

a neuronal gene because the Human Protein Atlas places it in a neuronal neurotransmission-

andsignaling expression cluster (Human Protein Atlas, “RILP”). RTN4RL1 has human

cerebralcortex and retinal expression and is classified as tissue enriched in cerebral cortex in

current Human Protein Atlas protein-summary metadata, but this does not establish V1 or

cortical-layer specificity (Human Protein Atlas, “RTN4RL1”). PITPNA and INPP5K remain

broadly available rather than demonstrably V1-selective.

       SERPINF1 retains trophic and vascular/glial opportunities in central tissue but does not

reproduce the same sharply localized RPE pattern in V1. WDR81 and RILP remain compatible

with endosomal and neuronal trafficking biology, yet Phase 3E found no evidence that WDR81-

RILP-RTN4RL1 form a synchronized adult V1 cell-type module. PRPF8 and RPA1 are even

less regionally discriminatory because their core functions in RNA processing and genome

maintenance make broad expression biologically expected.

       The adult cortical result therefore favors distributed vulnerability over a simple

visualcortex lesion model. The genes can be biologically active in cortex without being

selectively enriched in V1, layer 4, intratelencephalic neurons, corticothalamic neurons, or

another single cortical class. This distinction became the rationale for Phase 3F: if adult

specificity was weak, developmental timing might still reveal a period during which the deleted

genes are unusually relevant to visual-cortex formation.

Phase 3F: Human Visual-Cortex Development and Developmental Enrichment

Phase 3F initially centered on the developmental multiomic atlas of Wang and colleagues

       and was subsequently broadened to independent cortical arealization and spatial-

transcriptomic resources. Wang et al. analyzed 27 brain specimens and 38 biological samples

spanning five developmental stages from the first trimester through adolescence. Paired single-
                                                                                                  173

nucleus RNAseq and ATAC-seq were generated from PFC and V1, producing 243,535 nuclei

after initial quality control and a final cortical dataset of approximately 232,000 nuclei. The

study resolved progenitor, neuronal, glial, immune, and vascular classes and explicitly modeled

regional developmental differences (Wang et al.).

       The atlas is particularly valuable because it identifies a V1-specific layer-4

intratelencephalic trajectory rather than assuming that all layer-4 neurons are equivalent.

Common and V1-specific EN-L4-IT populations share an early trajectory and diverge later in

development. The authors reported 1,908 differentially expressed genes distinguishing

V1specific from common EN-L4-IT neurons and highlighted markers such as CUX1 and

KCNIP1. Their analysis used sample-level pseudobulk logic with age and dataset effects and

Benjamini- Hochberg multiple-testing correction (Wang et al.). None of the affected 17p13.3

loci is among the conspicuous published markers defining that V1-specific branch.

       A crucial evidence boundary was maintained throughout the Table 18 investigation. The

supplementary archive containing the full differential-expression table is publicly listed, but the

exact per-gene rows for all affected loci were not successfully retrieved in the research

environment. Consequently, Phase 3 does not label the deletion genes statistically nonsignificant

in that comparison. The justified statement is narrower: they are not among the highlighted

defining V1-specific EN-L4-IT markers, while their exact effect sizes and adjusted P values

remain an archival verification item.

       The broadened search materially strengthens the interpretation because independent

datasets show that V1 specialization is readily detectable when present. Bhaduri and colleagues

profiled ten major brain structures and six neocortical areas during peak neurogenesis and early

gliogenesis. They found extensive area-specific differential expression in the second trimester,
                                                                                               174

including in radial glia, with areal signatures becoming more pronounced as cells differentiate

toward excitatory neurons. Their results support strong frontal-versus-occipital molecular

patterning rather than a cortex that is transcriptionally homogeneous during development

(Bhaduri et al.).

        Qian and colleagues provide an even stronger spatial constraint. Using MERFISH and

complementary approaches, they constructed a spatial atlas exceeding 18 million fetal cortical

cells across eight cortical areas and seven developmental time points. They detected a discrete

V1-V2 molecular boundary by approximately gestational week 20 and found early

synaptogenesis-related changes in V1-specific layer-4 neurons (Qian et al.). Thus, the available

technology and datasets are capable of detecting visual-cortex-specific developmental programs.

The failure of the 17p13.3 interval to emerge as a conspicuous defining program cannot simply

be attributed to an inability of developmental transcriptomics to resolve V1. Developmental

Candidate: WDR81

        WDR81 remains one of the strongest early-development candidates in the interval, but

the evidence supports developmental opportunity rather than V1 specificity. Cross-sectional

developing-brain resources suggest prenatal cortical expression, and its established endosomal

biology gives that expression mechanistic relevance. However, WDR81 is not identified as a

major V1 arealization determinant in the Bhaduri, Wang, or Qian studies. The Phase 3F

classification is therefore: developmental neural relevance, suggestive to moderate; preferential

V1 specification, unsupported. Developmental Candidate: RTN4RL1

        RTN4RL1 is detectably expressed in human retina and brain and has a neuronal

cellsurface biology compatible with neurite and circuit maturation. BrainSpan and Allen

developmental resources provide temporal expression opportunity, while current Human Protein
                                                                                                  175

Atlas data show cerebral-cortex enrichment at the tissue level (Allen Institute for Brain Science;

Human Protein Atlas, “RTN4RL1”). The expression pattern is better described as

developmentally dynamic than as a simple prenatal peak. Because RTN4RL1 also suffers

nearcomplete canonical coding loss on the affected allele, it remains a high-priority mechanistic

candidate. Yet it is not established as a V1-specific arealization gene. Developmental Candidate:

RILP

       RILP retains a credible neuronal trafficking role but has weaker evidence for early

cortical specification. Its neuronal expression-cluster assignment supports central-neural

opportunity (Human Protein Atlas, “RILP”), while the developmental atlases do not identify it as

a major V1 arealization or layer-4 determinant. The most conservative interpretation is that RILP

may matter during neuronal maturation and endolysosomal cargo transport rather than during

initial specification of V1 identity. Developmental Candidate: SERPINF1

       SERPINF1 bridges the strongest retinal result and the developmental-cortical analysis. Its

RPE enrichment is direct, while a 2026 developmental-brain analysis reported a rapid increase in

SERPINF1 expression between approximately 24 gestational weeks and four months after birth

(Musante et al.). This observation is secondary to that study's primary disease question, so it

should not be over-weighted; nevertheless, it supports a developmental trophic opportunity

during the late-prenatal to early-postnatal transition. It does not establish V1 specificity.

Functional Ordering without a Demonstrated Molecular Chain

       The broadened developmental evidence permits a cautious temporal functional model:

WDR81 is most compatible with earlier endosomal and developmental regulation; RTN4RL1

with later structural, neurite, and circuit-maturation opportunity; and RILP with neuronal

endolysosomal transport during maturation. This ordering is an inference assembled from
                                                                                                176

independent functional and expression observations. It is not evidence that one gene activates the

next, that the genes form a shared transcriptional module, or that heterozygous loss produces a

sequential biochemical cascade.

       Wang et al. also inferred hundreds of enhancer-associated regulatory programs from

paired RNA and chromatin-accessibility data. The presence of a mapped regulatory landscape

makes a shared deletion-specific V1 regulon testable in principle. At present, however, no

published result establishes that the affected genes constitute a coordinated V1-specific

eRegulon. Regulatory hypothesis H6 therefore remains technically testable but unsupported at

the deletion-module level.

       Hypothesis Adjudication

       H1: Membrane/endosomal colocalization. Partially supported. The strongest celllocalized

evidence is the RPE-centered INPP5K-PITPNA-SERPINF1 configuration. A comparable

deletion-wide neuronal module has not been demonstrated in retina, dLGN, or V1.

       H2: Direct retinal localization. Supported for selected genes, especially INPP5K,

PITPNA, SERPINF1, PRPF8, TLCD2, and RTN4RL1 at differing levels of cellular resolution.

Direct localization does not establish pathological effect.

       H3: WDR81-RILP trafficking overlap. Mechanistically plausible but anatomically

unresolved. Adult retinal neuronal co-localization is not demonstrated; developmental evidence

is more compatible with temporally distributed roles.
                                                                                                 179

       H4: Developmental enrichment. Partially supported. Several genes have credible

developmental cortical opportunity, but the interval is not demonstrated as a V1-specific

developmental program.

       H5: Magnocellular specificity. Unsupported. Human dLGN transcriptomics does not

identify the affected loci as defining magnocellular markers, and no later developmental

evidence restores this hypothesis.

       H6: Shared V1 regulatory overlap. Presently unsupported. Modern multiomic atlases

make the question testable, but no shared affected-gene V1-specific eRegulon has been

established.

       Interpretive Synthesis: Developmental and Cellular Functional Reserve

       The most defensible lay and technical synthesis of Phase 3 is a functional-reserve model.

Several genes in the deletion are expressed in biologically relevant visual or neural

compartments, and some occupy complementary molecular systems. In RPE, the convergence is

unusually concrete because INPP5K, PITPNA, and SERPINF1 share cell-type opportunity while

contributing to phosphoinositide, lipid/membrane, and trophic biology. In the developing cortex,

WDR81, RTN4RL1, RILP, SERPINF1, PRPF8, and RPA1 provide different forms of

developmental or cellular opportunity, but not a single V1-specific transcriptional program.

       The term functional reserve is preferable to assuming a fixed fifty-percent loss of

function. A heterozygous deletion removes one genomic copy, but the downstream protein or

pathway effect depends on transcriptional compensation, protein stability, feedback regulation,

redundant pathways, cell type, developmental stage, and physiological demand. Phase 3

therefore supports the proposition that the deletion could reduce reserve in several molecular

systems; it does not quantify that reduction.
                                                                                              180

       Likewise, the project has not yet demonstrated a complete chemical or molecular chain

linking the deletion to a visual-neural phenotype. Individual biochemical functions are known:

INPP5K acts in phosphoinositide metabolism; PITPNA participates in

phosphatidylinositol/phosphatidylcholine transfer; WDR81 and RILP participate in

endosomal/lysosomal trafficking; SERPINF1/PEDF has trophic and lipid-linked retinal biology;

PRPF8 is a core spliceosomal component; RPA1 supports replication and genome maintenance;

and RTN4RL1 is a neuronal surface receptor-family protein. What remains unproven is the

connecting chain from heterozygous dosage reduction to altered pathway chemistry in the

relevant human visual cell, then to altered circuit function, and finally to a reproducible

phenotype.

       Negative Findings and Falsification Value

Phase 3 generated several negative findings that materially constrain the research. First,

       the deletion does not presently support a magnocellular-specific model. Second, adult V1

does not reveal a deletion-wide region- or layer-specific expression program. Third, the mature

retinal WDR81-RILP trafficking pair was not demonstrated as a shared neuronal module. Fourth,

the developmental interval genes do not emerge among the conspicuous V1-specific layer-4

markers in modern human developmental atlases. Fifth, no shared V1-specific regulatory

network among the affected genes has been established.

       These negative findings are not failures of the project. They reduce the number of

plausible mechanistic models and prevent the analysis from drifting toward a preferred

phenotype narrative. The strongest surviving model is more distributed: retinal/RPE

membranetrophic vulnerability combined with central-neural developmental, trafficking, RNA-
                                                                                                181

processing, and genome-maintenance constraints. This model is less anatomically simple but

better aligned with the evidence.

       Limitations

       The principal limitation is that expression is not perturbation. None of the reviewed

atlases directly measures the molecular state of cells carrying this specific heterozygous deletion.

Normal-reference expression establishes opportunity, not dosage response. A gene may be

highly expressed yet fully compensated after heterozygous loss, or modestly expressed yet

functionally dose-sensitive.

       Second, cell-resolved atlases have sampling and technical limitations. Single-nucleus

methods can underrepresent cytoplasmic transcripts, rare cell populations can be missed, and

regional dissections are imperfect. The dLGN study itself notes practical limitations in isolating

thin K layers (Bakken et al.). Spatial assays solve some localization problems but often measure

restricted gene panels or snapshots rather than complete developmental trajectories.

       Third, developmental datasets are generally cross-sectional across different donors.

Apparent age-related changes should not be interpreted as within-person trajectories unless the

analysis explicitly models age across samples. The older Allen/BrainSpan signals used in Phase

3F were therefore treated as supportive context, not definitive temporal kinetics.

       Fourth, the full gene-level statistics from Wang et al. Supplementary Table 18 remain

incompletely extracted for the affected loci. This prevents a formal statement that each gene is

statistically nonsignificant in the V1-specific versus common EN-L4-IT comparison. The correct

record is that none is among the conspicuous published defining markers and exact per-gene

statistics remain pending archival verification.
                                                                                                182

       Finally, the available evidence does not justify attributing Irlen Syndrome, visual stress,

photophobia, pattern glare, cortical hyperexcitability, epilepsy, retinal degeneration, or another

clinical phenotype to this deletion. If later phases address such phenomena, they should be

decomposed into measurable traits and tested against molecular and physiological data rather

than treated as a single genetically established syndrome.

Phase 3 Final Conclusion

Phase 3 establishes that the 17p13.3 deletion intersects multiple genes with credible

       spatial and temporal opportunity in the human visual system. The strongest direct

anatomical convergence is RPE-centered and involves INPP5K, PITPNA, and SERPINF1.

Additional genes contribute broader retinal, neuronal, cortical, developmental, or cellular-

maintenance opportunity. Human dLGN data do not support preferential magnocellular

enrichment. Adult cortical data favor distributed rather than V1-specific expression.

Developmental multiomic and spatial atlases demonstrate that V1-specific programs are

detectable, yet the affected interval does not presently emerge as a major defining V1

developmental module.

       The resulting evidence supports moderate mechanistic plausibility for a distributed

reduction in visual-neural developmental and cellular functional reserve. It does not establish a

direct dosage-to-chemistry-to-circuit-to-phenotype chain. Phase 4 should therefore shift from

expression opportunity toward perturbational evidence: whether heterozygous reduction of the

highest-priority genes measurably changes phosphoinositide handling, membrane composition,

endosomal trafficking, trophic signaling, RNA processing, genome-stress responses,

neurite/circuit maturation, or combinations of these processes in relevant human cellular models.
