Phase 5 - Casual Reader Summary

        What the genome research found, without the technical detail. FROZEN SCIENTIFIC

RECORD - VERSION 1.0. Prepared by Zachariah P. Laing, 29 September 2026.

This is a plain-language companion to the frozen Phase 5 scientific record. It simplifies

terminology but does not change the scientific conclusion.

The short version

        The deletion does not look biologically meaningless. It removes or disrupts a group of

genes that have real roles in the retina, brain development, nerve-cell communication, membrane

biology, cellular cleanup, and stress responses. The evidence does not point cleanly to one single

gene as the explanation, and it also does not support treating every deleted gene as an equal 50%

loss.

        The best overall explanation is that the deletion may reduce biological "reserve" across

several systems at once. In this model, many individual effects can be mild, partly compensated,

or only become important under stress. That fits the available research better than a single-gene

explanation or a simple sum of seventeen harmful effects.

        The original question involved photosensitivity and visually triggered seizures. Phase 5

does not prove that connection. Instead, it shows that broader eye and brain phenotypes are

currently better supported than photosensitivity itself.

What appears most worth paying attention to

1. Retinal/RPE functional reserve - This is the strongest eye-related signal. Several deleted

   genes affect photoreceptors, retinal support cells, cilia, phagocytosis, or retinal maintenance.

2. Brain development and neural-network organization - Several genes affect neurites,

   synapses, neuronal survival, trafficking, or coordinated network activity.

3. Stress-dependent vulnerability - A recurring pattern is that cells may look relatively normal

   at baseline but become less resilient under injury, oxidative stress, heavy activity, aging, or

   other challenge.

4. Seizure susceptibility / epileptogenesis - There is some direct epilepsy-related evidence,

   especially around MIR22, but it does not show that this exact one-copy deletion causes

   epilepsy.

5. Retinal timing / visual signal processing - Plausible, but not directly measured in the exact

   CNV.

6. Cortical synchronization / flicker entrainment - Mechanistically relevant, but currently a

   missing link rather than a demonstrated phenotype.

7. Photosensitivity / photoparoxysmal response - Still plausible, but it sits furthest downstream

   and currently has the least direct evidence of the major candidate outcomes.

Why one gene is probably not the whole story

       Some genes in the deletion have very strong eye or brain effects when they are

completely knocked out or heavily damaged. The problem is that the actual CNV is

heterozygous: one copy is affected and one copy remains. For several genes, one copy is enough

to avoid the severe phenotype seen in full knockouts.
                                                                                                 247

       PRPF8 is a good example: reduced RNA does not necessarily mean reduced protein.

PITPNA and WDR81 show threshold or recessive behavior, where the severe phenotype appears

mainly after much greater loss of function. That makes it risky to claim that any one of these

genes automatically explains the whole CNV phenotype.

Why "all the genes add up" is also too simple

       Not every deleted gene pushes biology in the same direction. In some experiments, losing

TLCD2, MIR22HG, or SERPINF2 can actually be protective under specific stress conditions.

RTN4RL1 loss can reduce coordinated neural activity rather than increase it. These findings do

not make the genes irrelevant; they show that their effects depend on context.

       That is why the leading model is a distributed reserve model rather than a simple

arithmetic total of gene losses.

What the simulations actually contributed

       The simulations were not used to invent biological evidence. They were used to answer

methodological questions: how many biological replicates would be needed, how easily small

effects could be missed, how often frequency scans generate false positives, and whether a

distributed mechanism could be mistaken for a single-gene mechanism.

   The final standardized simulation suite used 20,000 replicates per scenario/cell where

    applicable, totaling about 1.88 million primary stochastic draws/resamples.

   With that many runs, Monte Carlo sampling error became very small; the larger uncertainty

    now comes from biology, not from the number of simulations.

   Small one-copy effects can be difficult to detect with too few independent cell lines or

    clones.

   Using many cells from the same clone does not replace independent biological replication.
                                                                                                248

   Scanning many flicker frequencies without correction can produce apparently significant

    results by chance surprisingly often.

   Small datasets can make a distributed mechanism look like a simpler single-gene

    explanation.

So what does Phase 5 actually conclude?

       The deletion is best viewed as a candidate distributed visual-neural reserve lesion, not as

a proven single-gene photosensitivity mutation.

       The strongest candidate consequences are subtle retinal/RPE dysfunction, altered neural

development or network organization, and reduced resilience under stress. There is also

moderate support for an effect on seizure susceptibility or epileptogenesis. Photosensitivity

remains possible, but the key physiological chain from the retina through the thalamus and visual

cortex to a photoparoxysmal response has not been directly measured for this exact CNV.

What has NOT been proven

   That every deleted gene loses exactly half of its function.

   That the genes act synergistically.

   That the exact CNV causes a measurable retinal abnormality.

   That the CNV causes abnormal visual-cortex excitation or flicker entrainment.

   That the CNV causes a photoparoxysmal response or visually triggered seizure.

   That one specific gene is the sole driver.

Why the result is still useful

       The project started with a broad question about photosensitivity. Phase 5 narrowed the

problem. It now appears more defensible to study the CNV as a broader visual-neural reserve

problem, with retinal maintenance, neural-network organization, and stress response as the
